# Georgia Legal & Licensing Constraints

**Read this before writing a single line of marketing copy.**

Tino operates in **Georgia**, so everything here is Georgia law. That turns out to be good news for
research quality — Georgia's statutes answer the critical questions explicitly rather than leaving
them to interpretation — and bad news for one item on the service list, because Georgia's answer is
the strictest possible one.

Statutes cited are the Official Code of Georgia Annotated (O.C.G.A.); rules are Ga. Comp. R. & Regs.
Nothing here is legal advice. Two items at the end genuinely need an attorney.

**The three things that matter most:**

1. He **cannot** self-perform electrical or plumbing in Georgia, and **advertising** them is itself
   the violation. Not a gray area — the statute says it outright.
2. He **cannot** advertise sewer, storm drain, or water main work until he holds a Georgia utility
   contractor license. Same mechanism: offering the service is what makes you an unlicensed
   contractor.
3. **His old employment agreements may legally restrict which customers the site can market to.**
   Georgia enforces non-competes and lets judges narrow overbroad ones rather than void them. This is
   the most urgent item in the whole project and it needs a lawyer.

## 1. Electrical and plumbing: Georgia settles it outright

Both the "residential contractor" and "general contractor" definitions —
[§ 43-41-2(9)](https://law.justia.com/codes/georgia/title-43/chapter-41/section-43-41-2/) and
§ 43-41-2(5) — contain the same sentence:

> "…any work contractually undertaken by a [residential/general] contractor in the nature of
> **electrical contracting, plumbing, conditioned air contracting, low voltage contracting, or
> utility contracting** which falls within the licensing requirements of Chapter 14 of this title
> **may not be performed by the [residential/general] contractor but shall only be performed by a
> person who is duly licensed to perform such work under Chapter 14.**"

There is no self-perform option and no dollar minimum. § 43-41-17(f) confirms the specialty-contractor
exemption never authorizes unlicensed Chapter 14 work.

**He may still contract for it** as part of a larger scope — he just has to subcontract the trade work
to a Chapter 14 licensee. And under Rule 553-9-.01(t), knowingly hiring an *unlicensed* Chapter 14 sub
is a disciplinary offense against **his own** license, so vetting subs is a legal duty rather than
good practice.

### Advertising it is the violation, independent of doing it

This is the part that catches people. Georgia defines the trades by what you *offer*:

- **§ 43-14-2(7)** — an "electrical contractor" includes anyone who "bids for, offers to perform,
  **purports to have the capacity to perform**, or does perform electrical contracting services."
- **§ 43-14-2(11)** defines "master plumber" the same way.
- **§ 43-14-8(a)–(b)** then prohibits acting as either without a license.

So a services list containing the word "Electrical" makes him an unlicensed electrical contractor by
definition. Enforcement runs through § 43-14-11 injunctions (obtainable by the division, a county or
municipal inspection authority, **or the prosecuting attorney**, and expressly in addition to criminal
prosecution) and § 43-14-6(a)(4) discipline.

### What he can and cannot advertise

| Can self-perform and advertise | Must be subcontracted to a Chapter 14 licensee |
| --- | --- |
| Drywall, framing, carpentry, roofing | Electrical |
| Tile, paint, finishes | Plumbing |
| Kitchen and bath remodel carpentry | HVAC / conditioned air |
| Additions, carports, outdoor kitchen structures | Low-voltage |
| Masonry, concrete, hardscape, counters | Utility contracting (see §3) |
| On-site stormwater conveyance and detention that stays wholly within the project site and connects to no public or private system — second paragraph of § 43-41-2(4) | |

**Copy that works:** "We coordinate all electrical and plumbing through licensed Georgia trade
contractors, as required by O.C.G.A. § 43-41-2." Accurate, protective, and it reads as
professionalism rather than as a limitation. Note the small win in that last table row — genuine
on-site stormwater work he *can* self-perform, which is adjacent to his specialty.

## 2. Georgia does NOT require the license number in advertising — but print it anyway

A correction to the earlier multi-state draft. I read the full chapter index of Department 553
(Organization, Examination Exemption, Qualifications, Procedural Rules, Fees, Written Warranty,
Definitions, Disciplinary Action, Inactive License, Changes in Status, Renewal/CE). **There is no
advertising chapter.** Unlike California (B&P § 7030.5) or Arizona, Georgia imposes no
license-number-display requirement on residential or general contractors. Several contractor-licensing
lead-gen sites blur this; they're wrong.

**We should still put it in the header on every page.** It's the single most credibility-building line
available, only 3 of the 21 sites we studied do it, and it's what homeowners, GCs, and public owners
look for. It's now a marketing decision rather than a compliance one — which means we get to choose
placement on the merits.

**Two Georgia rules do touch documents:**

- **§ 43-41-14** — a building permit application must include his license number and the qualifying
  business entity in a sworn written document. An inspector who issues a permit without it commits a
  misdemeanor.
- **§ 43-41-17(l)** — he is *not* required to list his Chapter 14 subcontractors' license numbers on
  the face of a bid or proposal.

**If he later holds a Chapter 14 license (likely — see §3), display becomes mandatory.**
**§ 43-14-9** requires the registration number displayed conspicuously at the place of business,
prominently on commercial vehicles used in daily operations, in advertising, and **printed on all
invoices and proposal forms**. The statute's advertising list names yellow pages and newspapers and
predates the web, so websites aren't textually covered — but once he has that number, putting it on
the site is the obvious extension and clearly the intent.

## 3. The utility contractor license: his biggest opportunity, and a live constraint

Georgia licenses underground utility work separately, and **his municipal pipe experience maps
directly onto it.** Full path in
[02a-georgia-credentials-roadmap.md](02a-georgia-credentials-roadmap.md) — the short version here is
the legal constraint.

**What triggers it — § 43-14-2(17):** a "utility system" is any system **at least five feet
underground** installed or accessed by trenching, open cut, or cut and cover, "including… water supply
systems, and **sanitary sewerage and drainage systems**."

**There is no dollar threshold, and it applies to private developer work as much as municipal work.**
§ 43-14-8.4(c) goes further: no utility system may be constructed, altered, or repaired unless a
certified utility manager or utility foreman **is present at the job site**.

And the same definitional trap as electrical: **§ 43-14-2(14)** defines "utility contractor" to include
one who "bids for, offers to perform, **purports to have the capacity to perform**" utility
contracting. § 43-14-8.2(h) makes it unlawful for anyone to contract with a person they know isn't
licensed for it.

> **So: no sewer, storm drain, or water main services on the site until the license is in hand.** Until
> then, the commercial page describes his **experience and role** — "served as superintendent on
> municipal sanitary sewer and storm drainage installations" — which is true, valuable, and not an
> offer to perform.

One useful adjacency: **§ 43-14-2(12)** — a master plumbing *or* utility contractor license covers
plumbing systems "from the property line up to but not within five feet of any building," regardless
of cost or depth.

## 4. "Certified" and "licensed" are the two riskiest words on the site

The brief describes Tino as "certified." In Georgia that word carries real exposure from two
directions:

- **§ 43-41-12(a)(2)** — a **misdemeanor**, punishable by a fine of **not less than $1,000 or three
  months' imprisonment, or both**, for anyone who "falsely represents, **advertises**, or holds
  himself… out as a residential contractor or commercial general contractor licensee duly authorized
  to perform work under such classification of licensure." § 43-41-12(a)(5) covers using an expired or
  revoked license.
- **Georgia's Fair Business Practices Act**, § 10-1-393(b)(5) — unlawful to represent "that a person
  has a sponsorship, approval, status, affiliation, or connection that he or she does not have."
  Subsections (b)(2) and (b)(3) specifically reach confusion as to "**certification**." Remedies
  include **mandatory treble damages for intentional violations** plus attorney's fees, and
  § 10-1-393.5 singles out "home repair work or home improvement work" for a criminal penalty.

**Rules for copy:**

- Never write bare "certified." Always name the credential, the issuer, and the scope — e.g. "Level 1A
  Erosion & Sediment Control Certified, Georgia Soil and Water Conservation Commission."
- Never write "licensed" until a license is actually issued. No "license pending," no boilerplate
  "licensed and insured."
- **Do not advertise anything requiring a Chapter 41 license until he holds one.** § 43-41-2(3) defines
  "contracting" to include "**the offering of contracting services and the negotiation of or bid or
  proposal for engagement or a contract**." Offering *is* contracting. Additions, remodels, and
  framing over $2,500 are squarely Chapter 41.
- Don't advertise beyond his classification. Residential-Basic is limited by structure type;
  General Contractor Limited Tier caps any single contract at $1,000,000 (Rule 553-4-.05(8)).

### The $2,500 threshold, and why it isn't a loophole

The number lives in the *definition* at § 43-41-2(9): a "residential contractor" is only one whose
work or compensation "**exceeds $2,500.00**." Below that, the chapter doesn't apply. Note the general
contractor definition at § 43-41-2(5) has **no dollar floor**.

Separately, **§ 43-41-17(g)** exempts repair work at any dollar amount, provided he (a) **discloses to
the owner that he holds no Chapter 41 license** and (b) the work does not affect structural integrity.

**Neither is an advertising exemption**, and **§ 43-41-12(a)(7)** specifically criminalizes intentional,
repeated manipulation of contract values to dodge licensure. Don't structure the site around
sub-$2,500 slicing.

**§ 43-41-17(b)** is the commercial consequence and it's brutal: a contract requiring a license,
entered into by an unlicensed contractor, is "**unenforceable in law or in equity by the unlicensed
contractor**," and "**no lien or bond claim shall exist in favor of the unlicensed contractor**." He
could do the work and have no legal way to collect.

## 5. URGENT — his old employment agreements may restrict who the site can market to

**This is the item I'd move fastest on, and it needs a Georgia employment attorney, not us.**

Georgia was historically hostile to non-competes. The **Georgia Restrictive Covenants Act of 2011**
([O.C.G.A. § 13-8-50 et seq.](https://law.justia.com/codes/georgia/title-13/chapter-8/article-4/))
reversed that and made Georgia relatively employer-friendly. Two features make the usual "it's
probably too broad to enforce" instinct wrong here:

- **§ 13-8-53(d) — Georgia courts blue-pencil.** A court "may modify a covenant that is otherwise void
  and unenforceable so long as the modification does not render the covenant more restrictive." An
  overbroad covenant is not dead; a judge can narrow it and enforce the remainder.
- **§ 13-8-57 — timing presumptions.** Against a former employee, any restraint of **two years or
  less is presumed reasonable**. Five years or the payment period if he held equity or a profit
  participation.

**§ 13-8-53(a)** does limit who a non-compete binds — it's unenforceable against an employee who
doesn't customarily solicit customers, make sales, satisfy the executive test (managing a department,
directing two or more employees, hire/fire authority), or serve as a key employee or professional.
**A superintendent or project manager on municipal pipe networks very likely satisfies that test**, so
a non-compete against him would probably clear the category hurdle.

The bigger issue is narrower and more likely:

> **§ 13-8-53(b) — customer non-solicitation.** He can be bound from soliciting customers with whom he
> had "**material contact**," and **no geographic limit is required** for such a clause to be
> enforceable.
>
> For Tino, "customers with material contact" means **specific municipalities, counties, water and
> sewer authorities, developers, and general contractors** — exactly the entities the commercial side
> of this site is designed to attract. A valid non-solicit could bar him from approaching, say, DeKalb
> County Watershed Management or Gwinnett County DWR.

**That's not a copy problem, it's a market-definition problem.** It could determine whether the
commercial page can target metro Atlanta water and sewer authorities at all.

Also assume a **perpetual confidentiality obligation**: § 13-8-53(e) imposes no time or geographic
limit on trade-secret and confidentiality duties for as long as the information stays confidential.
For someone leaving a large construction firm, the classic trade secrets are **bid pricing, unit-cost
databases, estimating templates, subcontractor and supplier pricing, and municipal client relationship
files**. And § 13-8-58 lets successors and assigns enforce, so an acquisition doesn't extinguish
anything.

**What he needs to pull and have reviewed, before launch:**

- [ ] Every employment agreement, offer letter, and handbook acknowledgment from prior employers
- [ ] Any non-compete — check duration against the two-year presumption
- [ ] Any customer non-solicitation clause — **and a list of every agency, municipality, developer, and
      GC he had material contact with**
- [ ] Any NDA or trade-secret clause
- [ ] Whether he ever held equity or profit participation (raises the presumptive limit to five years)

## 6. Photos, privacy, and prior-employer work

**Georgia has no right-of-publicity statute** — it's entirely common law, and Georgia was the first
state to recognize a common-law right of privacy
(*Pavesich v. New England Life Ins. Co.*, 122 Ga. 190 (1905), itself an unauthorized-advertising case).

The controlling case for our purposes is ***Cabaniss v. Hipsley***, 114 Ga. App. 367 (1966), which
recognized **appropriation** — "the appropriation, for the defendant's benefit, use or advantage, of
the plaintiff's name or likeness" — and found liability where a photograph was published in an
advertisement **by mistake**.

> **Intent is not a defense in Georgia.** "We didn't mean to publish it" does not help. Written consent
> before publication is the only reliable protection.

*Martin Luther King, Jr. Center v. American Heritage Products*, 250 Ga. 135 (1982) confirms that
appropriating a name or likeness "without consent and for financial gain of the appropriator" is a tort
in Georgia.

**Own property — safe, and unusually strong.** His own house (doubled square footage), the 4-car
carport, and the outdoor kitchen with roof, sink, and stove are his property, shot by him, on his own
land. **This should carry the launch portfolio.** It's genuinely fortunate that his best proof is also
his least encumbered.

**Client property — get a written release.** No Georgia statutory form exists; a short written release
is enough. Photographing an occupied home's *interior* also implicates the intrusion-upon-seclusion
branch of *Cabaniss*. Put an express photo/marketing license clause in his contract. Frame to exclude
house numbers, mail, license plates, family photos, and anything revealing a security system layout.

**Prior-employer work — assume unusable.** Beyond the confidentiality exposure in §5, naming a former
employer's projects as portfolio work risks **FBPA § 10-1-393(b)(1)** (passing off services as
another's) and **(b)(3)** (confusion as to affiliation) if it implies he was the contractor rather
than an employee.

> **Default: describe the pipe-network experience in role terms.** "Served as project superintendent on
> municipal sanitary sewer and storm drainage installations" — not "our projects." That phrasing is
> accurate, avoids the FBPA problem, and is what a municipal buyer searches with anyway.

## 7. Two Georgia requirements that most contractor sites miss

Both are compliance obligations we can turn into differentiators.

### The mandatory written warranty — Ga. Comp. R. & Regs. R. 553-7-.01

A licensed **residential** contractor "**shall offer a written warranty**" on every "covered contract"
— any contract to construct or manage construction of a single-family residence where the value or
compensation **exceeds $2,500**. The warranty must describe, at minimum:

1. Covered work and activities
2. Covered exclusions
3. **Standards for evaluating work, which "shall be those set forth in the current edition of the
   Residential Construction Performance Guidelines as published by the National Association of Home
   Builders"**
4. The warranty term, with commencement date or event
5. Claim procedures
6. Contractor response options (repair, replace, or compensate)
7. Assignable manufacturer warranties

**Rule 553-7-.01(4):** a complete copy — or an identical blank standard form — **must be attached to
the contract before execution**, or otherwise made available for review.

Very few small Georgia contractors do this. Publishing the warranty terms on the site is simultaneously
a compliance win and one of the strongest trust signals available (only 7 of 21 sites we studied stated
a warranty term at all).

### The three-day cooling-off period for in-home signing

Two overlapping regimes, both applying to contracts signed in a customer's home:

- **O.C.G.A. § 10-1-6** — the buyer may cancel a home solicitation sale **until midnight of the third
  business day** after signing. All deposits refunded within **ten days**; the seller may retain the
  lesser of 5% or $25 as liquidated damages.
- **FTC Cooling-Off Rule, 16 C.F.R. Part 429** — three business days for home sales of $25 or more. The
  **seller must disclose the right to cancel at the time of sale and provide two copies of a
  cancellation form.**

> Per the Georgia Attorney General's Consumer Protection Division, **if the seller fails to provide the
> Notice of Cancellation form, the buyer may cancel at any time** — the clock never starts. Saturday
> counts as a business day.

Exclusions include emergency home repairs and sales at the merchant's regular place of business. **If
Tino signs remodel contracts in customers' living rooms, he must hand over the written notice and two
cancellation forms at signing.** This is the most commonly violated rule in residential remodeling and
it's free to comply with.

### Lien practice — a tool, not an obligation

Georgia imposes no "Notice to Owner" language requirement on a general contractor's contract. It runs
the other way: **§ 44-14-361.5(b)** — within **15 days** of physically commencing work, a **Notice of
Commencement** shall be filed with the superior court clerk (by the owner, the owner's agent, **or the
contractor**) and **a copy posted on the project site**. It must name the construction lender; omitting
that has excused claimants from giving Notice to Contractor.

Filing it is *his* protective tool: it forces lower-tier claimants to identify themselves within 30
days. Nothing about it belongs on the website — it belongs in his operating checklist.

| Step | Georgia specifics |
| --- | --- |
| LLC formation | Secretary of State **eCorp** portal. Articles of Organization ~**$110** ($100 + a $10 service charge effective Sept 6, 2025). Georgia street-address registered agent required. |
| Annual registration | **$60**, due **January 1 – April 1** (§ 14-11-1103(a)). $25 late penalty; failure means administrative dissolution. **No franchise or net-worth tax** on pass-through LLCs. |
| EIN | IRS, free |
| Contractor license | See [02a-georgia-credentials-roadmap.md](02a-georgia-credentials-roadmap.md) |
| **Sales & use tax number** | **Mandatory.** Register via **Form CRF-002** before any construction activity. |
| Business license | **Georgia has no statewide general business license.** He needs a local **occupation tax certificate** from the *one* jurisdiction where his office sits (§ 48-13-6 — the tax attaches to "locations or offices," not to every county he works in). Confirm home-occupation zoning if working from home. |
| General liability | Board-mandated minimums: **$300,000** Residential-Basic; **$500,000** Light Commercial, GC, and GC Limited Tier |
| Workers' comp | Required at **3 or more** employees (§ 34-9-2(a)(2)) — **and LLC members count** |
| Surety bond | **Not required statewide.** But § 43-14-12 lets local inspection authorities require bonds, and public work triggers payment/performance bonds under Georgia's Little Miller Act. |

### Sales tax: he is the consumer, and this surprises people

**§ 48-8-63(b)** and **Rule 560-12-2-.26(1)** — a contractor who furnishes tangible personal property
and performs services improving real property "**is deemed to be the consumer of all tangible personal
property used**" and pays tax at purchase.

The Georgia Department of Revenue's Contractor FAQ is explicit: "**ALL contractors, resident,
nonresident, general and subs, must register for a sales and use tax number**," and "**Contractors must
file a sales and use tax return on a monthly basis even if there is no sales or use activity.**" That
holds even for work performed for government agencies.

**So he does not itemize and collect sales tax from homeowners — he pays it at the register and prices
it into the job.** But he must register and file monthly regardless. Easy to miss, annoying to
unwind.

### Workers' comp: the "three employees" rule has a trap

The threshold is 3 or more regularly in service. But the State Board of Workers' Compensation states
plainly that for an LLC, "**the officers or members are included in the employee count**" — different
and less favorable than the sole-proprietor rule.

**§ 34-9-2.1** lets a member elect exemption by filing **Form WC-10** (maximum five members). The trap
is **§ 34-9-2.1(a)(3)**: an employer already subject to the chapter "shall remain subject… without
regard to the number of exemptions filed."

**§ 34-9-8** is why this matters commercially: a principal or intermediate contractor is liable for
compensation to employees of *his subcontractors*, and where the immediate employer has fewer than
three employees the claim may be presented **directly against the upstream contractor**. That's exactly
why GCs demand a certificate from every sub.

Penalties run **$500–$5,000 per occurrence** civilly, and willful failure to secure coverage is a
**misdemeanor** punishable by a **$1,000–$10,000** fine or up to 12 months.

**Practical answer: carry a policy or file a properly executed WC-10, and be able to email a
certificate the same day.** The board requires proof at application and as a condition of renewal
regardless of the three-employee minimum.
