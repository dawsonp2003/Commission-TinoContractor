# Legal & Licensing Constraints

**Read this before writing a single line of marketing copy.** Two findings here can change the
site's service list and page layout, and one of them carries criminal exposure in several states.

Nothing here is legal advice. Every item is state-specific, and we do not yet know Tino's state.
Treat this as a list of questions to confirm with his licensing board or an attorney.

---

## 1. BLOCKER: we may not be able to advertise electrical or plumbing

The brief says Tino "can do anything from drywall to roof to electrical to plumbing." Being *able*
to do the work and being *licensed to advertise and sell* it are different things, and in most
states the trades are licensed separately from general contracting.

| State | Rule |
| --- | --- |
| Florida | [§489.113(3)](https://www.flhouse.gov/Statutes/2019/0489.113/): a contractor "**shall subcontract** all electrical, mechanical, plumbing, roofing, sheet metal, swimming pool, and air-conditioning work" unless separately certified in that trade. |
| California | A B (General Building) licensee may only touch electrical as "incidental and supplemental" to a larger project, and **may not advertise** electrical services without a C-10. |
| Texas | Electrical is licensed by TDLR, plumbing by TSBPE. A GC self-performing either unlicensed commits a **Class C misdemeanor**. Both agencies accept competitor tips. |
| North Carolina | An NCLB general contractor license does not authorize trade work; separate NCBEEC (electrical) and NCBPHFSC (plumbing) credentials are required. |

California goes further: [§7027.1](https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=BPC&sectionNum=7027.1)
makes it a **misdemeanor to advertise for work outside your licensed classification**, with a
mandatory $700–$1,000 fine. The website *is* advertising.

**What this means for the build.** Until we have his exact license classifications in hand, we do
not publish "Electrical" or "Plumbing" as standalone service pages. Two safe framings:

- Licensed for it: name the classification next to the service (e.g. "Electrical — C-10 #XXXXXX").
- Not licensed for it: "We coordinate licensed electrical and plumbing subcontractors as part of
  full remodels." This is honest, still communicates turnkey capability, and is legal everywhere.

This is question #1 on the intake questionnaire. **Do not let the service list get written around
it.**

## 2. The license number probably has to be on the page, not in the footer

Several states require the license number in *all advertising*, and at least one specifies where.

- **California** [B&P §7030.5](https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=BPC&sectionNum=7030.5)
  — the number must appear in all construction contracts, subcontracts, calls for bid, and **all
  forms of advertising**. Websites and social profiles count. Civil penalty $100–$1,000 for a first
  offense (CCR §884). Vehicle lettering has its own size minimums (§7029.6, §7029.5).
- **Arizona** [A.R.S. §32-1124(B)](https://www.azleg.gov/ars/32/01124.htm) — the number must be
  prefixed "ROC" and appear on the jobsite, all written bids and estimates, and all published
  advertising. ROC
  [Substantive Policy Statement 2021.02](https://roc.az.gov/files/2021.02%20Online%20Advertising%20%26%20Prominent%20Display%20of%20License%20Number.docx%20%281%29.pdf)
  requires the business name and ROC number **directly on the home page — not in the footer** —
  unobstructed by graphics, at an appropriate font size. Unlicensed advertisers must state the exact
  phrase "Not a Licensed Contractor."

**Design consequence:** put the license number in the header or hero trust strip on every page, and
repeat it in the footer. This is the safest pattern across all states, satisfies Arizona's stricter
placement rule, and — as it happens — is also the single strongest trust signal available (see
[04-common-patterns.md](04-common-patterns.md), where only 3 of 21 real sites did this).

## 3. Word choice: "licensed, bonded & insured" and "certified"

- Use "Licensed, Bonded & Insured" only if all three are literally true and current. Bonded is the
  one most often claimed without a bond in place.
- **"Certified" is a term of art.** Florida, for instance, legally distinguishes a *certified*
  contractor (state-wide) from a *registered* one (local). The brief describes Tino as "certified" —
  we need to know what credential that maps to before we print the word.
- Avoid superlatives that imply a credential he doesn't hold ("master electrician," "engineer").
  "Engineer" is itself a protected title in many states.

## 4. Photos: the prior-employer problem is the sharp edge

Tino's municipal pipe-network experience is his rarest asset, and it is also the material we are
least likely to be allowed to show.

**Own-property work — safe, and unusually strong.** His own house (doubled square footage), the
4-car carport, and the outdoor kitchen with roof, sink, and stove are his property, shot by him, on
his own land. No release needed, no client to ask, and they are complete documented narratives.
**This should carry the launch portfolio.** It is genuinely lucky that his best proof is also his
least encumbered.

**Client work — needs a signed release.** Under US copyright law the photographer owns the image, so
if Tino shot it he owns the copyright. But copyright is not permission to publish pictures of
someone's private home; performing the work grants no implied marketing license. Get a written
release at project closeout naming the platforms (website, social, print) and confirming that no
address, client name, or contact details will be published. Frame to exclude street numbers, mail,
family photos, vehicles with plates, and open closets.

**Prior-employer work — assume we cannot use the photos.** Images taken while employed may be the
employer's property, and municipal and commercial jobs are routinely covered by NDAs or
confidentiality clauses. Breaching one is a contract problem, not just a copyright problem.

> **Default position: describe the pipe-network experience in words, publish no photos of it without
> written clearance from the former employer.** Prose costs us nothing here — "twelve years
> installing sanitary sewer and storm drainage for a heavy civil contractor, pipe up to X inches,
> for agencies including Y" is exactly the language a municipal buyer searches with anyway.

## 5. Reviews and testimonials

- The FTC rule on consumer reviews and testimonials applies to anything we publish. No fabricated
  reviews, no undisclosed incentives.
- Google added a guideline on **July 24, 2026** banning fake or undisclosed incentivized reviews
  both on-page and in structured data, with manual action as the penalty. If Tino ever offers a
  discount in exchange for a review, it must be disclosed.
- Separately, do **not** mark up his own reviews with `aggregateRating` — see
  [06-leadgen-and-local-seo.md](06-leadgen-and-local-seo.md#structured-data--three-things-changed-recently).
  We can display them; we just can't claim stars in search results.

## 6. Business formation checklist

The brief mentions an LLC is planned but not yet named. Rough order of operations, all of which
produce artifacts we need for the commercial track:

1. LLC formation with the Secretary of State
2. EIN from the IRS
3. State contractor license — plus any trade-specific licenses (see §1)
4. General liability insurance ($1M per occurrence / $2M aggregate is the common floor)
5. Workers' compensation (or a documented sole-proprietor exemption)
6. Commercial auto
7. Inland marine / tools & equipment coverage
8. License or surety bond (amount set by state)
9. Sales tax permit where materials are taxable
10. Local city or county business license
11. Business bank account and bookkeeping — needed later for prequalification financials

**Name consistency matters more than it looks.** The LLC name, the name on the license, the Google
Business Profile name, and the site's footer should match exactly. Mismatches hurt local SEO
(NAP consistency) and, in California, the license must be advertised under the name it was issued
to.
