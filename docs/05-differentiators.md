# High-Impact Differentiators

Things that almost no contractor site has, that clearly add value or trust, and that Tino is
unusually well positioned to do. Ranked by impact-to-effort for *his* situation specifically.

The theme running through all of them: **specifics beat adjectives.** Every site in the study that
worked did so by publishing something a competitor couldn't copy without changing how they operate.

---

## Tier A — Do these. They're the reason the site will work.

### 1. Print the license number in the header, on every page

Only 3 of 21 sites do it. It costs nothing and it immediately separates him from the unlicensed
operators undercutting him on price. Da Vinci's `WA #606007438` and Valverde's `#276469,
classifications A, B, C10, C16, C36` are the model — note that publishing the *classification codes*
is even stronger, because it proves scope rather than just claiming it.

**Georgia does not require this** — unlike California or Arizona, there's no advertising rule for
residential and general contractors
([detail](01-legal-and-licensing.md#2-georgia-does-not-require-the-license-number-in-advertising--but-print-it-anyway)).
So this is purely a trust play, which means we place it wherever it works hardest: the header, visible
on every page. If he later holds a Georgia utility contractor license, § 43-14-9 makes displaying
*that* number mandatory on vehicles, invoices, and proposals anyway.

### 2. A trust strip in the hero, not on an About page

One horizontal row, above the fold, on every page: Google rating and review count, project count,
license number, years of experience, warranty term. Da Vinci's exact pattern. Five facts, one
glance, no scrolling.

The reason this works is that it front-loads exactly what homeowners say they weigh — references
(64%), scope experience (47%), licensing (26%) — instead of making them dig for it.

### 3. Split the homepage into two journeys

**Homeowners** and **Commercial / Municipal**. Different heroes, different proof, different first
action:

| | Homeowners | Commercial / Municipal |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Hero leads with | Before/after photos, reviews | Pipe-network experience, self-performance |
| Proof | Named testimonials, warranty, permits | License classifications, insurance limits, safety certs, equipment |
| First action | Tap-to-call, 4-field form | Download capability statement, email an estimator |
| Tone | Warm, reassuring, plain | Procurement-literate, technical |

Nothing off-the-shelf does this, and it's where Tino's differentiation lives. See
[04-common-patterns.md](04-common-patterns.md#serving-both-audiences-on-one-site) for the patterns
observed.

### 4. Real photos of his own work, with before/after sliders

He has unusually clean material here: he doubled his own house's square footage, built a 4-car
carport, and built an outdoor kitchen with roof, sink, and stove. Those are **complete, documented,
personally-owned narratives** — no client permission needed, no NDA risk, no former-employer
ownership question. It's genuinely fortunate that his most impressive proof is also his least
encumbered.

Label each by scope, location, duration, and trades involved. Zero stock photos — over half of
contractor sites use them and it reads as fake instantly.

Before/after sliders are the one interaction worth the JavaScript. Vendor-published claims about
their conversion lift (PipelineOn: 40–60% longer time-on-page; TradeCalcs: 20–30% better form
submissions) are marketing material and shouldn't be trusted as data — but the verifiable point
stands: **almost no small contractor has one**, and Tino's before/after material is exceptional.

### 5. A downloadable commercial capability statement

**Nobody in the 21-site sample hosted one.** A GC's estimator who can download Tino's credentials at
9pm will shortlist him over someone who has to be emailed and waited on. Full spec in
[07-commercial-bid-playbook.md](07-commercial-bid-playbook.md).

### 6. Lead the municipal page with self-performance and procurement vocabulary

His large-scale sewer, storm-drain, and water-main experience is his rarest asset and the hardest
thing for a homeowner-focused competitor to fake. KE&G's register is the model: CMAR, design-build,
JOC, IDIQ, hard bid — that's the vocabulary a municipal engineer searches with, and using it
correctly signals "we've been through your procurement process" faster than any amount of prose.

Pair it with what he personally self-performs. Publishing a percentage ("75% self-performance") is
the civil-side equivalent of a portfolio, and Valverde's "we self-perform all traffic control, no
subcontractors, faster mobilization" shows how to turn it into a benefit rather than a stat.

### 7. Make the response-speed promise explicit, and keep it

DECA publishes a "documented 24–48 hour response time." Valverde runs a 24-hour emergency line
*separate* from the main number. Intent decays fast and most competitors are slow, so a specific,
kept promise is a real differentiator — but only if he can keep it. See
[06-leadgen-and-local-seo.md](06-leadgen-and-local-seo.md#speed-to-lead) on why the famous "5-minute"
statistic is weaker evidence than its ubiquity suggests, and
[02-needed-from-tino.md](02-needed-from-tino.md#on-9-specifically) for what we need to ask him.

## Tier B — Strong, worth pushing for

### 8. Publish price tiers

The biggest single differentiator found, and only 2 of 21 sites do it. Three tiers per service —
budget / mid / high — each with a dollar range, a timeline, and a scope list, plus a "last updated"
date so it ages honestly.

Three separate benefits: it pre-qualifies leads so he stops driving to estimates he can't win, it
captures the "how much does a kitchen remodel cost" searches that competitors cede to national
aggregators, and it directly answers the #2 and #3 homeowner complaints (unclear proposals 34%,
pricing 39%).

The strongest version isn't the numbers, though — it's Alpha's *policy*: showing clients actual
material receipts instead of a bundled line item hiding a 20–35% markup. If Tino works that way, say
so plainly. A competitor can't match it without changing their business.

### 9. A permits-and-process page

9 of 21 sites have a numbered process; almost none combine it with permit transparency. Name the
Georgia jurisdictions he pulls permits in, walk through what inspections happen, and explain that
unpermitted work can void a homeowner's insurance.

This reframes his licensing from a cost into the reason to hire him — which matters specifically
because his cheapest competition is unlicensed. And it's the same credibility argument that
transfers straight to the commercial side.

Georgia adds a real hook here: **§ 43-41-14 makes it unlawful for a building inspector to issue a
permit without the contractor's license number** in a sworn document. So "I can pull the permit" is a
verifiable claim about his licensure, not a soft promise — and an unlicensed competitor structurally
cannot make it.

### 9b. Publish the warranty — because Georgia already requires him to offer one

**Ga. Comp. R. & Regs. R. 553-7-.01** requires a licensed Georgia residential contractor to *offer* a
written warranty on every single-family contract over $2,500, benchmarked to the NAHB *Residential
Construction Performance Guidelines*, attached to the contract before signing.

Almost no small Georgia contractor does this. Since he has to produce the document anyway, publishing
its terms on the site converts a compliance obligation into one of the strongest trust signals
available — only 7 of 21 sites stated a warranty term at all. Free differentiation.

### 10. Bilingual as substance, not a badge

DECA's framing is the model: bilingual service as a core operating model, not an occasional
accommodation. Combine with DNA's approach of one genuinely native Spanish page rather than a
half-maintained mirror, and put a **WhatsApp** link next to the phone number — standard in
Spanish-language construction marketing, nearly absent from US contractor sites.

And work the commercial angle too: Spanish fluency is a crew-coordination credential to a GC, not
just a customer-service feature.

### 11. Named testimonials with role and organization

Only 2 of 21 sites attribute testimonials to a named person with a title. On the commercial side
this is transformative — KE&G's quotes come from named staff at Tucson Water and the Town of
Clifton. Full paragraphs beat one-liners (Potter's run several sentences and describe 10-month jobs).

Best of all is a testimonial that answers the fear the buyer actually has. "They don't push for
change orders when it's not justified" does more work than any number of five-star ratings.

### 12. Productized small jobs

Ace sells half-day (4hr) and full-day (8hr) packages. It removes the "will he even quote my small
job?" hesitation entirely and gives a price-anchored entry point. Worth considering for the handyman
end of Tino's range — but only if he wants that work. Ask first.

## Tier C — Later, or only if he asks

- **Video walkthroughs and drone footage.** High impact, high effort, and nobody at his scale does
  it. Even phone-shot walkthroughs would stand out. Defer to post-launch.
- **Free 3D renderings before commitment.** Two sites lead with this. Strong for larger remodels,
  but it's a services-and-tooling commitment, not a website feature.
- **Client project portal with real-time updates.** Two sites have one, and clients praise it
  unprompted in testimonials. Genuinely differentiating, but it's an app, not a page. Phase 3+.
- **Financing.** One site has a whole page on it (Hearth, 17 lenders, soft pull). Matters for large
  remodels; premature for a new LLC.
- **Referral program.** Cheap to add, and word of mouth is already his likely top channel (64% of
  homeowners cite recommendations).
- **Three-axis project filtering** (scope / service / sector, per MAREK). Right idea for someone with
  this many trades, but it needs a large project library first. Revisit at 30+ projects.
- **Equipment list.** Civil contractors sell these as proof of capability. Add it to the commercial
  page once we know what he owns.
- **Subcontractor portal.** Only relevant if he grows to hiring subs.

## Explicitly not doing

- **Rotating hero sliders.** Replaced by a single hero image in every modern recommendation.
- **Animated stat counters.** The most commonly broken element in the entire study (`0 Project
  Complate`). Static numbers.
- **The award-gallery aesthetic** — WebGL, parallax, scroll-jacking. Hurts a buyer on one bar of
  signal comparing three bids.
- **A blog, at launch.** 8 of 21 have one and most are dormant award posts. A stale blog is worse
  than none. Revisit only if he'll actually feed it.
- **`FAQPage` structured data.** The rich result is fully deprecated — see
  [06-leadgen-and-local-seo.md](06-leadgen-and-local-seo.md#structured-data--three-things-changed-recently).
  Write the FAQ content anyway; skip the markup.
- **AI chatbot.** Recommended by several 2026 trend pieces. For a one-person business where the
  differentiator is *reaching Tino directly*, a bot that intercepts that is a downgrade.
