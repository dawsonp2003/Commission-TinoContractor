# Photo Guide

Send this to Tino. Photos are the whole site — a contractor site with real photos beats a prettier one
with stock photos every time, and over half of contractor sites use stock.

**A phone camera is completely fine.** Don't buy anything, don't hire anyone.

---

## The shot list

### Must have

| Shot | Why |
| --- | --- |
| **Before + after, same position** | The single most valuable thing you can send. Lets us build a slider people drag back and forth. Almost no contractor at this level has one. |
| **Tino working, face visible** | Only ~4 of 21 sites we studied had real photos of the actual person. People are hiring *him*, not a logo. |
| **A portrait** | For the About page. Doesn't need to be formal — on a jobsite is better. |
| **Detail close-ups** | Tile edges, trim joints, a clean electrical panel, finished corners, caulk lines. These prove finish quality far better than wide shots. |
| **Wide "hero" shot of each finished project** | One strong image per project for the gallery tile. |

### Strongly wanted

- **Progress / in-work photos.** Framing open, subfloor exposed, trench dug, rough-in visible. These
  prove he actually did the work rather than just finished someone else's. Nobody has these.
- **The four flagship own-property projects**, in depth: the house addition, the 4-car carport, the
  outdoor kitchen with roof and sink and stove, the interior renovations.
- **Video.** Even a phone walkthrough of a finished room while talking. Almost nobody at this scale
  does video.
- **Equipment and truck**, for the commercial page.
- **Certifications and license** — a clear photo of the actual cards.

## How to shoot it

**Before/after is the one thing worth being deliberate about.**

1. Before you start a job, stand somewhere memorable — a doorway, a corner, next to a fixed feature.
2. Take the photo. Note where you stood.
3. When it's done, **stand in exactly the same place** and shoot again.
4. Same height, same direction, same lens (don't zoom on one and not the other).

That's it. If the two photos line up, the slider effect is dramatic. If they don't, it's just two
photos.

**General tips:**

- **Horizontal (landscape) for rooms and exteriors.** Vertical is fine for tall details.
- **Turn lights on and open the blinds.** Phone cameras need light more than anything else.
- **Shoot from a corner** for interiors — it makes rooms look their real size.
- **Hold the phone at chest height**, level. Tilting up or down bends the walls.
- **Outside: overcast days are better than bright sun.** Harsh shadows hide detail. Early morning or
  late afternoon if it's sunny.
- **Clean up first.** Move the tools, coil the cords, take the trash out. Two minutes of tidying is
  worth more than any editing.
- **Take way more than you think you need.** 10 shots of one room is normal. We'll pick.
- **Send originals, not screenshots or texted copies.** Texting compresses them. AirDrop, Google
  Drive, Dropbox, or a shared album keeps full quality.
- **Don't edit or add filters.** Send them raw, we'll handle it.

## What to keep out of frame

Some of this is politeness and some is legal.

- **House numbers and street signs** — don't publish where a client lives
- **Mail, packages, prescription bottles, documents** — anything with a name on it
- **Family photos, kids' artwork, personal items** on walls and fridges
- **License plates**
- **Open closets, bathrooms mid-use, bedrooms with personal belongings out**
- **Neighbors' property**
- **Other companies' logos** on equipment or signage
- **Anything that looks like a code violation or unsafe practice** — no missing fall protection, no
  removed guards. A GC's safety manager will notice, and so will a homeowner's inspector.

## The permission rules

This is the part that matters legally. For **every** photo, we need to know three things:

**1. Whose property is it?**

- **Tino's own property** → free to publish. No permission needed. *This is why the house addition,
  carport, and outdoor kitchen are the ideal launch portfolio.*
- **A client's property** → we need a signed OK from the client. We'll provide a simple form. Getting
  it at the end of the job, while they're happy, is far easier than calling back a year later.

**2. Who took the photo?**

If Tino took it, he owns the copyright. If someone else did (a client, a coworker, a photographer),
we need their permission too.

**3. Was it work done for a previous employer?**

> **Assume these photos are not usable until proven otherwise.** Photos taken while employed may be
> the employer's property, and municipal and commercial jobs are routinely covered by NDAs or
> confidentiality clauses. Breaching one is a contract problem, not just a copyright question.

For the large pipe-network jobs, **we plan to describe the experience in words instead**, which
carries the same weight with a municipal buyer — that audience searches by system type, pipe size, and
agency, not by photo. If Tino can get written clearance from a former employer, great; we just won't
depend on it.

## Making it a habit

The photos matter after launch too. Google rewards recently-added photos on a Business Profile, and a
site whose newest project is two years old looks like a business that stopped operating.

A routine worth building:

1. **Before photo** at the start of every job, from a memorable spot
2. **One progress photo** somewhere in the middle
3. **After photo** from the same spot, plus two or three details
4. **Ask for the review and the photo release together**, at closeout, while they're happy

Four photos and one ask per job. That's the whole habit, and it compounds.

Put each project in its own folder, named so we can tell what it is:

```

  before-01.jpg
  before-02.jpg
  during-framing-01.jpg
```

to and we'll sort it out. Getting the photos matters far more than getting them organized.
