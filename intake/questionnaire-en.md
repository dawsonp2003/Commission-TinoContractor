## 1. The important legal stuff (please do this part first)

We looked up the Georgia rules specifically. A few of them have real teeth — advertising something
you're not licensed for in Georgia is a misdemeanor with a minimum $1,000 fine — so we want to get
this right rather than guess.

- What **Georgia city or county** is your business based out of?
- **Do you have a Georgia contractor license?** If yes: what class (Residential-Basic, Residential-
  Light Commercial, General Contractor, or GC Limited Tier), what's the number, and when does it
  expire? A **photo of the license** is the easiest way to send this.
- If you don't have one yet, no problem — that's normal for starting out, and we've mapped out how to
  get it. It does change what the website can say at first, so we just need to know.
- **When you say "certified," what does that mean exactly?** Which certification, from who? In Georgia
  that word is legally loaded, so we want to name the actual credential instead of just saying
  "certified."
- Do you have a **Georgia sales and use tax number**? (Georgia requires every contractor to have one
  and file monthly, even in months with no activity. It catches a lot of people out.)

### Electrical and plumbing — this one's already decided

We know you can do this work, and we're not doubting that. But Georgia law is unusually blunt here, so
this isn't really a question: **a Georgia general or residential contractor is flat-out prohibited from
self-performing electrical or plumbing.** It has to go to a licensed trade contractor, no matter how
small the job. And in Georgia, *advertising* that you do electrical or plumbing is itself the violation
— even if you never touch it.

So what we need to know is just:

- **Do you hold a Georgia electrical or plumbing license?** (Master Plumber, Electrical Contractor
  Class I or II.) If so, that changes things and we'd love to know.
- If not — and this is the normal answer — we'll word the site as: *"We coordinate all electrical and
  plumbing through licensed Georgia trade contractors."* That's accurate, it's legal, and it still
  tells people you handle the whole project start to finish.

Nothing about this limits the business. It just changes six words on a page.

### Now the good news: your pipe experience is worth a Georgia license

This was the best thing we found. Georgia licenses underground utility work separately — sewer, storm
drainage, water mains, anything five feet or deeper — and **your background is exactly what that
license is designed for.** Very few contractors who do drywall and tile also hold it.

The path is: a 12-hour safety course → **Utility Foreman** (one year of experience, **no exam**) →
**Utility Manager** (exam) → a **Utility Contractor license** for your LLC. Mostly based on experience
rather than school.

Two things we need from you on this:

- **Do you already hold any Georgia utility license or certificate?**
- **Can you start requesting notarized experience letters from your old employers — this week?**

That second one is the most time-sensitive thing on this whole list, so it gets its own section below.

### Business setup

- Is the **LLC formed** yet? What's the exact name on it?
- Does that name match your license exactly? (Even small differences cause problems.)
- Do you have an EIN?
- **Insurance:** do you carry general liability? What are the limits? Workers' comp, or are you exempt
  as the owner? Commercial auto? Who's your agent?
  - Heads up: Georgia's licensing board requires **$300,000** minimum general liability for
    Residential-Basic and **$500,000** for the other classes. If you're shopping for a policy anyway,
    buy the $500,000 — it covers you for every license class so you don't have to re-shop later.
- Do you have a **local occupation tax certificate** from your city or county? (Georgia has no
  statewide business license, but your local jurisdiction probably wants one. Just the one where your
  office is — not every county you work in.)
- Are you **bonded**? By who, for how much? (Georgia doesn't require it statewide, but public work
  does.)

## 1b. Two urgent asks — please start these before anything else

Both of these get harder the longer you wait, and neither depends on the rest of this questionnaire.

### A. Dig up everything you signed with your old employers

We need to see any **non-compete**, **non-solicitation agreement**, or **NDA** you signed — employment
agreements, offer letters, handbook acknowledgments, anything.

Here's why this matters, honestly: Georgia is one of the stricter states about enforcing these. And
unlike a lot of places, **a Georgia judge can rewrite an agreement that's too broad instead of throwing
it out** — so the usual "that'd never hold up" assumption doesn't apply here.

The specific concern is a clause about not soliciting your former employer's customers. For you, those
"customers" would be the cities, counties, water and sewer authorities, developers, and general
contractors you dealt with — which are exactly the people we'd want the website to attract. If such a
clause exists and is valid, it could legally limit who we're allowed to market to.

- Can you find and send those documents?
- Separately: **which agencies, cities, counties, developers, and GCs did you deal with directly** at
  your old jobs? Not for the website — for the lawyer, so they can tell us what's off-limits.
- Did you ever hold **equity, shares, or a profit share** in a former employer? (That changes the
  rules.)

We'd want a Georgia employment attorney to read these before the site goes live. Better a small legal
bill now than a letter from a former employer later.

### B. Ask your old employers for notarized experience letters — now

To get the Georgia utility license, the state requires **notarized letters on company letterhead from
each employer** confirming your experience. Not a resume — actual letters from them.

Each letter needs:

- Company letterhead, and notarized
- Your dates of employment
- Your role and title
- What kind of utility work it was — sanitary sewer, storm drainage, water mains
- **Specifically that the systems were at least five feet underground** (that's the exact thing the
  state looks for)

**Why now:** HR departments reorganize, supervisors retire, companies get bought, records get purged.
Every month makes this harder. And frankly it's easier to ask a former boss for a favor *before* you're
out there competing with them.

**Without these letters the utility license application can't even be filed.** So this is the single
highest-value thing you could do this week.

### Pricing (optional, but this could be your biggest advantage)

We looked at 21 contractor websites. Only **two** posted any prices. The ones that do get better
leads, because people who can't afford it don't call, and people who can already know they can.

If you're up for it, for each of your main services: a **rough range** (like "$8K–15K for a typical
bathroom"), roughly **how long** it takes, and what's **included**. Even "starts around $X" helps.

Totally your call — if you'd rather talk price in person, we'll leave it off.

## 4. Your projects

This is the part that actually sells the work. **Aim for 8–12 projects.** Eight good ones beat thirty
thin ones.

**Start with your own place** — the addition where you doubled the square footage, the 4-car carport,
the outdoor kitchen with the roof and sink and stove, and the renovations. Those are perfect, because
it's your own property, so there's nobody to ask permission from.

There's a template at [`project-template.md`](project-template.md), but honestly a voice memo per
project works fine. For each one we want to know:

- What was it, and what did you actually do? (List the trades — "framing, roofing, electrical rough-in,
  drywall, tile")
- How big? Square footage, number of rooms, whatever fits
- How long did it take?
- What city, what year?
- Roughly what did it cost, or a range? (Optional)
- **Was there a problem you had to solve?** Something unexpected, something tricky, something another
  guy would've done wrong? *This is the most interesting part and the part everybody leaves out.*
- Did you pull permits? Which city?
- Would the customer be willing to be a reference or leave a review?

### One important thing about photos

For each set of photos, we need to know **three things**:

1. Did **you** take the photos?
2. Whose property is it — yours, or a customer's?
3. **Was this work you did for a company you worked for?**

That last one matters. Photos from jobs you did while employed somewhere may legally belong to that
employer, and municipal and commercial work is often under a confidentiality agreement. We don't want
to cause you a problem, so if it's employer work we'll **describe it in words instead** — which
honestly works fine for the big pipe jobs anyway.

For customer jobs, we'll need a quick signed OK from them. We can give you a simple form.

## 5. Photos

**20–40 photos minimum.** Real ones, from your phone. Never stock photos — more than half of
contractor websites use fake photos and people can tell instantly.

Full guide with tips: [`photo-guide.md`](photo-guide.md). Short version:

- **Before AND after, from the same spot.** This is the single most valuable thing you can send. We
  can build a slider people drag back and forth. Almost no contractor at your level has this.
- **Photos of you working.** Face visible, on a real jobsite. Only about 4 out of the 21 sites we

- One decent photo of you for the About page
- **Close-up detail shots** — tile edges, trim, a clean panel, finished corners. These prove your
  finish quality way better than wide shots.
- Any video at all, even just walking through a finished room while talking. Almost nobody does this.

This part is about getting hired by **companies and cities** rather than homeowners. Your pipe network

### The pipe work, in detail

- Which companies did you work for, and what years?
- What kinds of systems? Sanitary sewer, storm drainage, water mains — all of it?
- **How big?** Pipe diameters, materials, depths, lengths, project dollar values.
- Which **cities, counties, or agencies** was the work for? Can we name them?
- **What was your actual role?** Laborer, operator, foreman, superintendent, designer? Be specific —

### Certifications — send photos of any cards you have

- **Georgia Level 1A Erosion & Sediment Control** (the "blue card")? — *Georgia requires this for
  anyone doing land-disturbing work, which trenching is. It's the cheapest and fastest credential on
  your list and we'd suggest getting it first if you don't have it.*
- OSHA 10 or OSHA 30?
- Competent person for trenching/excavation?
- Confined space entry?
- Traffic control or flagger?
- NASSCO PACP/MACP/LACP (the pipe camera inspection one)? — *Georgia cities often require this for
  camera work, so it's worth getting if you don't have it*
- Welding? Backflow?
- Anything else, even expired

### Equipment and capacity

- What equipment do you **own**? Excavator, skid steer, trailer, compactor, laser level, pipe laser?
- What can you rent or get access to?
- What do you do yourself vs. sub out? (Companies like to see a number here, like "I self-perform 80%")
- Do you have a **bonding** relationship with a surety company? Any capacity letter?
- Do you keep books? QuickBooks or similar? Do you have a CPA? (Bigger jobs require financial
  statements — smaller ones usually don't, so this isn't urgent.)
- References from the commercial side — old supervisors, city contacts, general contractors?

### Getting on Georgia bid lists — one thing that's easier than expected

We found a useful shortcut. To bid Georgia DOT work as a prime contractor on jobs **up to $2 million**,
you only need to be **registered as a Subcontractor** with GDOT — not fully prequalified. That's a much
lighter process, and $2M is far above anything you'd bid starting out. It's probably the single best
registration to get.

Also worth registering with: Team Georgia Marketplace (the state's bidding portal, free), and directly
with the metro Atlanta water and sewer authorities — Atlanta Watershed Management, DeKalb Watershed,
Cobb County Water, Gwinnett DWR, Clayton County Water Authority.

*One caveat: we should check your old non-solicitation agreement before approaching any agency you
worked with before. See section 1b.*

### One heads-up on minority business certification

You may have heard that being Hispanic gets you automatic access to set-aside government contracts.
**That changed.** Federal courts struck down the automatic racial presumption in 2023, the SBA formally
removed it effective September 2026, and the DOT did the same for its DBE program in October 2025.
Right now DBE contract goals are actually **suspended** while they re-review everyone already
certified.

There are still real options in Georgia:

- **DBE** through GDOT, or through MARTA if you're in Fulton, DeKalb, or Clayton county. Free, but it
  now needs a written personal statement rather than just checking a box.
- **City of Atlanta** has a Hispanic American Business Enterprise (HABE) category and sets
  participation goals on city contracts.
- **NMSDC** private-sector MBE certification through the Georgia Minority Supplier Development Council.
- **HUBZone** — purely based on your address, nothing to do with ethnicity, and free to look up.

We'll dig into this properly. Just wanted you to hear it from us rather than from someone selling
outdated advice. Our honest read: the credentials worth building on are the ones based on skill and
size — the utility license, the blue card, GDOT registration — because those don't depend on how the
court cases turn out.

Questions for you: do you own 51%+ of the business? Are you interested in pursuing certification?
